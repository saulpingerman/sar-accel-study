\begin{abstract}
A TPU is cheap only on dense bfloat16 matrix products, and SAR backprojection is a gather. Whether spotlight image formation can be written in the TPU's idiom, and what it then costs against a GPU and a CPU, was measured on three Umbra collections at native size, formed on a TPU v5e, a TPU v6e, an Nvidia L4 and a 16-vCPU AMD instance by exact backprojection, factorized backprojection and polar format, with every image scored against a float64 exact backprojection. The factorized algorithm runs in kernels written for each accelerator (Pallas on the TPUs, CUDA on the L4). A thousand Panama images at float32 accuracy cost \$@@c.panama.l4.fp32.usd@@ on the L4, \$@@c.panama.v5e.high.usd@@ on the v5e and \$@@c.panama.v6e.high.usd@@ on the v6e, and @@concl.cpu.vs.l4@@ times the L4's figure on the CPU. The v6e is the fastest device, @@concl.v6e.vs.l4.fp32@@ times faster than the L4 per image at @@concl.v6e.price.vs.l4@@ times the hourly price. Float16 throughout the L4 pipeline raises the image error by @@l4.f16.loss@@~dB and lowers the cost by @@l4.f16.saving.pct@@\%; the TPUs' native single-pass products add @@sp.vs.3pass.db@@~dB of error, concentrated in phase errors beside bright returns that would be visible in a coherent product. Polar format costs \$@@c.panama.gpu-l4.pfa.fp32_taps_corr.usd@@ per thousand on the L4, @@pfa.vs.ffbp.l4@@ times less than factorized backprojection there, but its final resampling runs @@pfa.tpu.vs.l4@@ times slower on a TPU and its image carries a $-32$~dB residual whose source was not found.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A spotlight SAR processor now has a choice of hardware it did not have five years ago. The cloud rents GPUs and TPUs by the hour at prices set by the machine-learning market, and a phase history of $2 \times 10^8$ complex samples forming $10^8$ pixels is, on paper, the regular arithmetic those devices were built for.

The difficulty is that they were built for different arithmetic. A GPU is fast on the operation backprojection performs, an independent per-pixel loop that reads a range profile at a computed address. A TPU does that operation on its vector unit at a rate two orders of magnitude lower, and is fast only when the work is a dense matrix product with bfloat16 operands. The question for a shop deciding where to put its quota is therefore not which device has the higher peak rate. It is whether image formation can be rewritten in the TPU's idiom without losing accuracy, and what the rewritten algorithm then costs against a GPU running the natural one.

That question was put to three Umbra collections at the vendor's native size, formed on four instances by three algorithms and scored against a float64 exact backprojection. The answer is that the L4 forms the float32-accuracy image for \$@@c.panama.l4.fp32.usd@@ per thousand against \$@@c.panama.v5e.high.usd@@ and \$@@c.panama.v6e.high.usd@@ on the v5e and v6e, with kernels written for each accelerator and taken to measured fractions of each device's limits (Appendix~\ref{app:kernels}), and that the CPU costs @@concl.cpu.vs.l4@@ times the L4 (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). The v6e is the fastest device. What separates the accelerators beyond that is how each behaves when the arithmetic is lowered (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}), and polar format is the cheapest algorithm on the GPU and the slowest on a TPU (Section~\ref{sec:pfa}). An earlier internal round on smaller, simulated data is superseded here and is referred to below as the first round.

\section{Prior work}
\label{sec:prior}

Backprojection on parallel hardware has a fifteen-year literature, almost all of it on GPUs and almost none of it on cost. Fasih and Hartley gave the first single-GPU CUDA implementation~\cite{fasih2010}; Benson, Campbell and Cook scaled it to gigapixel images on a GPU cluster and reported throughput as a function of precision~\cite{benson2012}; Park and colleagues reached 35 billion backprojections per second per node on Xeon and Xeon Phi processors by strength reduction and locality optimization~\cite{park2012}; Cordes and Leeser ran the kernel on FPGAs with general-purpose hosts~\cite{cordes2009}. On the accuracy side, Capozzoli, Curcio and Liseno replaced the linear interpolation of the range profiles with a NUFFT and showed it more accurate at similar cost on a GPU~\cite{capozzoli2013}, and Xu and colleagues recently ran a streaming GPU backprojection in float16~\cite{xu2022}. These papers establish that the GPU is the natural home of the exact algorithm; none measures what an image costs in dollars, and none compares against a device that cannot gather.

The two approximate algorithms used here are older. Fast factorized backprojection descends from McCorkle's quadtree~\cite{mccorkle1996} and Ulander's formulation~\cite{ulander2003}, was applied to strongly nonlinear airborne tracks by Frey and colleagues~\cite{frey2009}, and was put on GPUs for circular SAR by Ponce and colleagues~\cite{ponce2011}. Polar format is the textbook algorithm~\cite{carrara1995,jakowatz1996}; its scene-size limit from wavefront curvature was derived by Doerry together with a post-processing compensation~\cite{doerry2007}, and derived again in terms of the residual quadratic phase error by Gorham and Rigling~\cite{gorham2016}. Jakowatz and Doren compared polar format with backprojection for spotlight images in 2006~\cite{jakowatz2006}. The polar format of this report follows that line: the image is formed fast and the known distortion is removed in a final resampling.

The TPU has been used outside machine learning only where a problem can be written as dense matrix products. Jouppi and colleagues describe the architecture and its matrix units~\cite{jouppi2017}, and the fourth generation's sparse cores for embedding lookups~\cite{jouppi2023}. Lu and colleagues formulated large discrete Fourier transforms as Kronecker products of dense matrices on TPU clusters~\cite{lu2020} and then the nonuniform FFT, reporting 20 to 80 times a CPU on one TPU card~\cite{lu2021}; Lewis and colleagues ran distributed dense linear algebra at supercomputer scale on TPU pods~\cite{lewis2022}. Those results are the reason the factorized algorithm here is written entirely as matrix products. What the literature does not contain is a measurement of SAR image formation on a TPU at all, or a cost comparison between TPU, GPU and CPU on real spotlight collections at native size against a common float64 reference. That is this report's contribution.

\label{sec:data}

Three collections from Umbra's open data catalog~\cite{umbra2023} were used (Table~\ref{tab:scenes}). % BEGIN GEN platforms
% END GEN platforms

Three algorithms were run (Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}). \emph{Exact backprojection} sums, for every pixel, the range-compressed pulse interpolated at the pixel's range~\cite{gorham2010}: one indexed read per pixel and pulse, @@sc.panama.lookups@@~trillion for the Panama image. A CUDA kernel runs it on the L4 with 8 times oversampled profiles in float32 or float16; a float64 CPU version with 16 times oversampling is the reference. On the TPU the same gather runs at $4 \times 10^7$ to $10^8$ reads per second depending on the access pattern, against about $10^{10}$ on the L4 (Appendix~\ref{app:tpu}), so exact backprojection was not run there.

\emph{Factorized backprojection} divides the image into tiles, re-centers the phase history on each tile with a phase ramp, filters and decimates it, and recurses~\cite{mccorkle1996,ulander2003}. Every stage is an element-wise or a dense matrix product, the operation the TPU's matrix units are built for. One JAX program runs on all four devices; it is the CPU form and the baseline on the accelerators. Where it is faster, strided convolutions replace the decimation matrices (\emph{conv. filters} in the tables), and the phase ramps are evaluated directly or from a coarse and a fine table (\emph{direct} and \emph{split-table} ramps).

The accelerator rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost} use kernels written for each device, Pallas on the TPUs and CUDA on the L4 (\emph{fused kernels} and \emph{CUDA kernels} in the tables), in which the rotated phase history of each child and the ramp matrices of the final stage stay on chip instead of passing through memory. Appendix~\ref{app:kernels} gives the kernels, the builds that led to them and their bounds against the units of each device; the images are the same as the JAX program's to within the rounding of the precision concerned. The tiles' band limits leave a residual against exact backprojection that is independent of the arithmetic (Table~\ref{tab:prec}, float64 row).

\emph{Polar format} resamples the pulses and samples onto a rectangular wavenumber grid by chirp-z transforms~\cite{rabiner1969} and forms the image by one FFT~\cite{carrara1995,jakowatz1996}; it is the cheapest of the three (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). It assumes a planar wavefront, so scatterers are displaced by an amount that grows with the square of their distance from the scene center; the final step resamples the image through the fitted displacement map (Appendix~\ref{app:pfa}). Its pulse resampling runs as a dense product or as a sum over gathered rows (\emph{gather}).

The TPU's matrix units multiply bfloat16 operands only. A float32 product is formed from one bfloat16 pass by default, which gives bfloat16 accuracy, or from three or six passes (JAX precision \emph{default}, \emph{high}, \emph{highest}), called single-, three- and six-pass products below. The L4's tensor cores take float16 operands (11 significant bits against bfloat16's 8), so \emph{sixteen-bit} means a more accurate type on the L4 than on the TPU. The L4's default float32 product uses TF32 operands (also 11 bits); the configurations called \emph{float32} on the L4 use full float32. Every configuration keeps the geometry in float32 or float64 and lowers only the storage type of the data and the operand type of the products; the CPU runs native float32 and float64 (Appendix~\ref{app:formats}).

\section{Measurement protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Device time per image is the wall time of one image on a warm device with the data resident, compilation excluded and the per-collection host work included (about 0.5~s). Throughput is the number of images per hour of a pipelined loop in which the transfers of the next collection and the previous image overlap the formation of the current one. Cost per image is the instance price divided by the throughput; where no pipelined loop was run, the inverse device time is used and the entry carries an asterisk. Repeated timings agree within 4\%, and the L4's pipelined loops run 6 to 11\% slower per image than its device time (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

Every image is compared with the float64 exact backprojection of the same collection after one gain has been fitted per algorithm family (complex for the backprojections, real for polar format). The error quoted is the energy of the difference relative to the energy of the reference (dB). The largest pixel difference, the 5 by 5 coherence and the amplitude and phase deviations over the brighter half of the pixels are tabulated with it. The selected configuration of each device, algorithm and precision is the fastest implementation form whose image is within 0.1~dB of the other forms on that device. Power was measured on the L4 only, from \texttt{nvidia-smi}.

The factorized errors on Melbourne and Iowa are within 1.6~dB of the Panama values of Table~\ref{tab:prec} (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). The reference's own convergence, measured on a crop at 8, 16 and 32 times oversampling, is @@ref.conv16@@~dB at 16 times, so no exact-backprojection error can be measured below about $-69$~dB. \subsection{Polar format}
\label{sec:pfa}

Polar format assumes a planar wavefront, and at these scene sizes the assumption is not good enough on its own. A scatterer at distance $\rho$ from the scene center is focused about $\rho^2/2R$ from where it belongs in range, which at $R = 744$~km (mid-aperture) is 4~m at 2.5~km, some ten pixels. The azimuth displacement is larger and depends on the squint: @@pfa.shift.panama.azm@@~m against @@pfa.shift.panama.rgm@@~m at the Panama corners. What saves the algorithm is that the error is almost pure geometry. The fit of Appendix~\ref{app:pfa} puts the corner displacement at up to @@pfa.shift.panama.az@@ pixels in azimuth and @@pfa.shift.panama.rg@@ in range, the measured positions of bright targets agree with it to 0.2~pixel, and the image is sharp everywhere. Its 5 by 5 coherence against the reference is only @@p.tpu-v6e.pfa.fp32.coh@@, but that is misregistration, not defocus.

Before its final resampling the image forms in @@t.panama.tpu-v6e.pfa.fp32.s@@ to @@t.panama.cpu-c4d16.pfa.fp32_taps.s@@~s depending on the device, the floor of what polar format can cost; its features sit up to eighteen pixels from their true positions at that point, so every polar-format figure in this report is for the resampled image.

The final resampling forms the image at twice the pixel density without carriers, resamples it at the apparent positions with a six-tap Lanczos kernel and restores the carriers (Appendix~\ref{app:pfa}). It brings the error to @@p.gpu-l4.pfa.fp32_corr.err@@~dB on Panama (@@p.melbourne.pfacorr.err@@ and @@p.iowa.pfacorr.err@@~dB on the other collections), the coherence to @@p.gpu-l4.pfa.fp32_corr.coh@@ and the registration to 0.001~pixel. The residual grows from $-40$~dB at the center to $-30$~dB in the outer quarter (Figure~\ref{fig:rings}). Its cause was not identified. Varying the resampling kernel and the raster oversampling changed it by less than 0.6~dB, and longer pulse interpolators made it up to 0.9~dB worse. The registration error and the reference's convergence are too small to account for it (Appendix~\ref{app:pfa}, which also notes one untested candidate).

The resampling is an indexed read at a computed position, 36 per pixel. On Panama it adds @@pfa.corr.l4.add@@~s on the L4 and @@pfa.corr.cpu.add@@~s on the CPU, so the image takes @@c.panama.gpu-l4.pfa.fp32_taps_corr.s@@~s on the L4 and @@c.panama.cpu-c4d16.pfa.fp32_taps_corr.s@@~s on the CPU, @@pfa.time.vs.ffbp.l4@@ and 19 times faster than factorized backprojection on the same device. On the TPUs it takes @@c.panama.tpu-v5e.pfa.fp32_taps_corr.s@@~s (v5e) and @@c.panama.tpu-v6e.pfa.fp32_corr.s@@~s (v6e), slower than factorized backprojection, because scattered gathers run on the TPU's vector unit at about $4 \times 10^7$ reads per second. For production use on a TPU the resampling would have to move to the host or be replaced by an approximation without scattered gathers (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\section{Fairness of the comparison}
\label{sec:fair}

The device comparison depends on each device having been run with an algorithm and arithmetic appropriate to it; Appendices~\ref{app:tpu} and \ref{app:protocol} give the measurements behind the following. On the TPU, a profile of the first factorized level shows the two decimation products taking @@prof.v6e.mm@@~ms per tile and the phase rotation before them @@prof.v6e.rot@@~ms, with the rotation moving 534~GB/s. \label{sec:concl}

The conclusions hold for the fastest form of each flow found in this study (Section~\ref{sec:fair} and Appendix~\ref{app:kernels}).

\begin{enumerate}
\item For routine factorized imaging at float32 accuracy, the L4 is the cheapest of the four machines. The L4 forms the image in @@c.panama.gpu-l4.ffbp.fp32_conv.s@@~s, @@concl.v6e.vs.l4.fp32@@ times the v6e's time, at @@concl.l4.price.frac@@ of its hourly price.
\item The v6e is the choice when latency matters. It forms the Panama image in @@c.panama.tpu-v6e.ffbp.fp32_high_direct.s@@~s with three-pass products, @@concl.v6e.vs.l4.fp32@@ times faster than the L4 in float32 at @@concl.v6e.price.vs.l4@@ times the hourly price, and it is on the Pareto front at no precision. Whatever it forms, the v5e forms at the same error for less, more slowly.
\item The saving from sixteen-bit arithmetic, and the error it introduces, differ by device and by product. The amplitude image is unchanged and the mean coherence stays at @@p.tpu-v6e.ffbp.fp32_fast_pallas2_direct.coh@@; the lowest coherence falls to @@p.tpu-v6e.ffbp.fp32_fast_pallas2_direct.cohmin@@ (@@p.tpu-v6e.ffbp.fp32_high_pallas2_direct.cohmin@@ with three-pass products) beside bright returns, where the phase error reaches @@sp.phmax.worst@@$^\circ$ (Melbourne; 86$^\circ$ on Panama). On the TPUs the scattered reads of its final resampling alone take 42 to 104~s, longer than the whole factorized image, so a TPU should not be used for polar format until that step is rewritten without gathers or moved to the host. The $-32$~dB residual is the open problem of this study. \end{enumerate}

\section{Algorithms}
\label{app:algorithms}

The phase history is motion compensated to the scene reference point. For pulse $p$ with antenna position $\mathbf{a}_p$ and frequency sample $f_k$,
\begin{equation}
S_{p,k} = \sum_n \sigma_n \exp\!\left(-j \frac{4\pi f_k}{c}\, \Delta R_p(\mathbf{x}_n)\right),
\qquad
\Delta R_p(\mathbf{x}) = \lVert \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{a}_p \rVert - \lVert \mathbf{a}_p \rVert ,
\label{eq:signal}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_n$ and $\mathbf{x}_n$ are the complex amplitude and position of scatterer $n$ and $c$ is the speed of light. The CPHD files give the transmit and receive positions per pulse, whose midpoint was used as $\mathbf{a}_p$, and the start frequency and sample spacing per pulse; the mean start frequency and spacing were used, which differ from the per-pulse values by at most @@data.griddrift@@ of a sample spacing. A Taylor weighting ($\bar{n}=4$, $-35$~dB sidelobes)~\cite{taylor1955} was applied along both axes in every algorithm. The image grid is the vendor's: pixel $(i, j)$ lies at $(i - n_x/2)\,s_x\,\mathbf{e}_1 + (j - n_y/2)\,s_y\,\mathbf{e}_2$ from the reference point, with $\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2$ the SICD column and row unit vectors and $s_x$, $s_y$ its sample spacings.

\paragraph{Exact backprojection.} Each pulse is range compressed by a zero-padded inverse FFT, with 8 times oversampling in the selected configurations and 16 times in the reference and in the two rows so marked, and each pixel is the sum over pulses of the linearly interpolated profile at $\Delta R_p$ times the carrier phase $\exp(j 4\pi f_c \Delta R_p / c)$~\cite{gorham2010}. The range difference is evaluated in the cancellation-free form
\begin{equation}
\Delta R_p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{q}{1 + \sqrt{1 + q / r_p}}, \qquad q = \frac{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2}{r_p} - 2\, \hat{\mathbf{u}}_p \cdot \mathbf{x},
\end{equation}
with $r_p = \lVert\mathbf{a}_p\rVert$ and $\hat{\mathbf{u}}_p = \mathbf{a}_p / r_p$. The reference implementation evaluates everything in float64 with Numba~\cite{lam2015} on the CPU. The CUDA kernel, compiled through CuPy~\cite{okuta2017}, evaluates the base range, the unit vector and the reciprocal range of each 32 by 32 pixel tile in float64 once per pulse, and the per-pixel remainder relative to the tile center in float32; the pulses are processed in chunks of 256 so that the range profiles of one chunk fit in memory at any image size. In the first round the per-pixel geometry was computed in float32 from the scene center and its error grew with the size of the scene; computed from the tile center the error does not grow.

\paragraph{Factorized backprojection.} The image is divided recursively into tiles, as in the quadtree~\cite{mccorkle1996,xiao2000} and fast factorized~\cite{ulander2003} backprojection algorithms. At each level the phase history of a parent tile is multiplied by $\exp\{j 4\pi f_k (\lVert \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{a}_p \rVert - \lVert \mathbf{c}' - \mathbf{a}_p \rVert)/c\}$ to move its reference point from the parent center $\mathbf{c}'$ to a child center $\mathbf{c}$; the child's signal is then band limited in frequency and in pulse index in proportion to the child's size, and both axes are low-pass filtered and decimated by a Kaiser-windowed sinc~\cite{kaiser1974} with 70~dB design attenuation. At the last level the range to each pixel is linearized about the tile center, the kernel separates in the two image coordinates, and the tile image is a product of two matrices followed by a per-pixel quadratic phase correction, so that no stage requires an indexed read. The implementation handles rectangular images of any size: the side of each level divides the padded image by factors chosen from 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3 and 2, with 32 by 32 pixel final tiles, and the decimation factors follow from the range and Doppler extents of a child tile. For the Panama image the three levels divide the azimuth side by 8, 8 and 6 and the range side by 8, 7 and 5, and the first level decimates the 14,399 samples by 6 and the 15,186 pulses by 4. The first two levels compute their phase ramps from float64 geometry on the host and the remaining level from float32 geometry on the device; in the first round a float32 second level was found to add an error that grew with the size of the scene. The ramps themselves are evaluated on the device in float32, either directly (\emph{direct ramps} in the tables) or as the product of a coarse and a fine table (the \emph{split-table} form, about $2\sqrt{n}$ sines and cosines per ramp of $n$ samples, used by every row not labeled \emph{direct ramps}); the device times of both forms are in Appendix~\ref{app:stats}. The decimation filters are applied as dense matrix products (the TPU form), as strided convolutions (the L4 and CPU form) or as explicit sums over taps; on the TPU the three give the same image to better than $-140$~dB, and on the L4 the convolution differs from the dense product by $-64$ to $-76$~dB with TF32 products and not at all with full float32 products. One first-level tile is processed per call to the device, which bounds the working memory to about 6~GB at these sizes on every device. Polar format is described in Appendix~\ref{app:pfa}.

\section{Using the TPU}
\label{app:tpu}

\paragraph{Survey.} In the first round the gather that backprojection needs per pixel and pulse was measured on the TPU at about $10^8$ reads per second; the L4's CUDA kernel performs the 1.6 trillion reads of the Panama image in 104~s, about $1.5 \times 10^{10}$ per second. The public documentation of the TPU generations, the JAX and Pallas documentation and the published work on irregular computation on TPUs were searched in October 2026 for a way to run exact backprojection, or any lookup-based formulation, at a rate comparable with the L4. A TPU core has a matrix unit, which multiplies dense tiles of bfloat16 operands at the chip's rated rate, and a vector unit, which executes element-wise operations and gathers at a rate set by memory bandwidth and lane count, two orders of magnitude lower per element. No texture or cache hierarchy for scattered reads is documented. The v6e and TPU7x add sparse cores for embedding lookups, which Google's Cloud TPU documentation (accessed October 2026) places near $10^9$ rows per second per chip and which are programmed through Pallas, JAX's kernel language; at that rate the @@sc.panama.lookups@@~trillion reads of the Panama image would take about 30 minutes, against 100 to 200 seconds on the L4. Writing the lookup as a one-hot matrix product, so that the matrix unit does it, multiplies by a matrix whose density is one in several thousand, and the arithmetic cost exceeds the dense algorithm's by the same factor. No published work was found that forms SAR, beamforming or tomographic images on a TPU; the nearest are formulations of FFTs and non-uniform FFTs as matrix products~\cite{barnett2019}, the approach taken here. The documentation's recommendations for dense workloads were applied and tested: shapes padded to multiples of 128 (v5e) or 256 (v6e), bfloat16 operands for default products, explicit precision per product and profiling of the compiled program; the recommendation for the TPU7x (two devices per chip) could not be tested.

\paragraph{Profile.} Figure~\ref{fig:profile} shows the time of each stage of the first level on one first-level tile of the Panama collection, 15,186 pulses by 14,399 samples decimated to 3,805 by 2,408, measured one stage at a time on the v6e with single-pass products and on the L4 with TF32 products (profiling one stage at a time materializes the inputs and outputs of each stage, which the fused program does not; for a whole tile these exceed the v5e's 16~GB, so the v5e was not profiled). The phase ramp fused with the complex multiplication (rotate) costs @@prof.v6e.rot@@~ms on the v6e; the frequency-axis decimation as a dense product @@prof.v6e.dk@@~ms, as a strided convolution @@prof.v6e.dkconv@@~ms and as a block-banded product @@prof.v6e.dkband@@~ms; the pulse-axis decimation @@prof.v6e.dp@@, @@prof.v6e.dpconv@@ and @@prof.v6e.dpband@@~ms. The dense products run at @@prof.v6e.tflops@@~TFLOP/s including the multiplications by the zeros of the filter matrices, and the rotation moves @@prof.v6e.rotgbs@@~GB/s through memory. On the L4 the same stages take @@prof.l4.rot@@~ms for the rotation and @@prof.l4.dk@@, @@prof.l4.dkconv@@ and @@prof.l4.dkband@@~ms for the three forms of the frequency-axis decimation, so the strided convolution was used on the L4 and the dense product on the TPU. \section{Kernels for the factorized algorithm}
\label{app:kernels}

The factorized rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost} are formed by kernels written for each accelerator after a stage-by-stage profile of the JAX program, that is, of the code XLA (JAX's compiler) generates from it; this appendix records the diagnosis, the kernels, the time each step removed (Table~\ref{tab:kernels}) and the bounds the final builds reach (Table~\ref{tab:bounds}). As one JAX program the factorized algorithm took @@kbase.v6e.3.s@@, @@kbase.v5e.3.s@@ and @@kbase.l4.32.s@@~s for the float32-accuracy Panama image on the v6e, v5e and L4 and cost within @@spread0.panama.fp32.pct@@\% across the three devices; the kernels made the TPUs @@kgain.v6e.3@@ to @@kgain.v5e.3@@ times faster at that accuracy and the L4 @@kgain.l4.32@@ times, without changing the images, and the comparison of the main text is between devices each running the fastest form found for it. Terms from Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms}: a level splits each parent tile's phase history into those of its children, the tiles of the next level; level 0 is the split of the whole collection into the first-level tiles, and the two levels after it are the device levels, whose geometry is computed on the device. The XLA program is the baseline. The plan, filters, geometry and arithmetic order are the same in every build, and each build's image was checked against the dense image at the same precision on a simulated scene before it was timed: single-pass and float16 builds agree with the dense image to the level of their own rounding, three-pass and float32 builds to below $-80$~dB.

\paragraph{Diagnosis.} On the v6e, one first-level tile of the Panama image took 51~ms through the XLA program with single-pass products: 8.3~ms in the first level, 13.3 in the second, 7.9 in the third and 21.4 in the final stage. The three levels are bound by memory traffic: XLA writes the rotated phase history of every child (24 bytes per sample, read back by the decimation product) at about 580~GB/s, a third of the v6e's 1,640~GB/s, and the children of the three levels total 63 billion samples per image. The final stage materializes the two ramp matrices of every 32 by 32 tile, $2T$ by $P_f Q_f$ each, through five or six element-wise passes before the one product that uses them, and its direct form evaluates 400,000 sines and cosines per tile. The same structure holds on the L4 at the convolution path's lower bandwidth.

\paragraph{TPU kernels (Pallas).} Two kernels, written in Pallas (JAX's kernel language for the TPU), replace the XLA stages. The level kernel takes a block of pulses and a window of columns of a parent into the core's vector memory and applies the phase ramps of up to eight children in turn. Each ramp is the product of a coarse table, one angle per 128-column chunk, and a fine table, one angle per column within the chunk; both are precomputed once per pulse and child by XLA, so the kernel evaluates no sines (Pallas lists them as its most expensive operations, and an earlier build that evaluated the coarse table in the kernel spent a third of its time on them). The rotated window is multiplied by the banded block of the decimation matrix on the matrix units, in one or three bfloat16 passes, and at the two device levels the pulse-axis decimation is accumulated on the matrix units across the pulse blocks in the same kernel, so neither the rotated window nor the frequency-decimated child reaches high-bandwidth memory; at level 0 the decimated child is too large for vector memory and the pulse product stays a separate dense product. Children of one parent share each load, the device levels batch the parents of a tile into one launch, and first-level tiles are formed in groups of sixteen so that the phase history itself is read once per group. Where one block of outputs covers a whole row (the device levels), the window is clipped to the data and read once; at level 0 consecutive output blocks overlap by the filter length.

The final kernel forms the $2T \times 2T$ product of Appendix~\ref{app:algorithms} ($T = 32$ pixels on a side, $P_f \times Q_f$ pulses by frequency samples per tile) on the matrix units, with both ramps generated by a complex recurrence along the frequency index $q$: for a pixel offset $d$ along an image axis and the component $u_p$ of the unit vector to the antenna along that axis, the ramp $\exp(-j 2\pi\, d u_p (a_0 + a_1 q))$, with $a_0$ and $a_1$ the cycles per meter at the first frequency sample and per sample step, is $X W^q$ with $X = \exp(-j 2\pi d u_p a_0)$ and $W = \exp(-j 2\pi d u_p a_1)$, so one tile needs four tables of sines and cosines of size $T \times P_f$ instead of one sine per sample. A first version of this kernel with one sine per sample was no faster than XLA (Table~\ref{tab:kernels}), which placed that stage on the transcendental unit; the recurrence version removed it. Three variants were measured and rejected (Table~\ref{tab:kernels}): building the level kernel's fine tables by the same doubling idea in XLA doubled the image time, because each intermediate table of fewer than 128 columns occupies full 128-lane tiles in TPU memory; evaluating the coarse-table sines over the used lanes only and padding afterwards cost 4 to 8\%, because XLA holds the table 128 lanes wide either way and the pad is a copy; and stacking four tiles into one 256 by 256 product in the final stage, to fill the systolic array, gained 3\% on the v6e at three-pass precision and nothing otherwise, and lost 2 to 4\% on the v5e, whose arrays are 128 by 128.

\paragraph{Bounds.} Table~\ref{tab:bounds} compares each stage with the bound set by the unit that binds it. The unit rates were measured on each device with microbenchmarks in the same framework (\texttt{ubench\_tpu.py}, \texttt{ubench\_gpu.py} in the repository): on the v6e, high-bandwidth memory copies at 1,391~GB/s; the vector unit executes the level kernel's own rotation mix on data resident in vector memory at 117 billion samples per second and a plain copy through vector memory at 155 billion, so the rotation runs within 1.3 times of the vector load and store path itself; XLA evaluates 57 billion sine and cosine pairs per second; the matrix units reach 457~TFLOP/s on a large bfloat16 product and 42 at the final stage's 64-row shape. Against these, on the v6e, level 1 runs at the vector unit's rate (ratio 1.0), level 0 at 1.4 times its vector bound with the unfused pulse product passing through memory, and level 2 at 2.5 times its largest bound but at 0.9 of the sum of the terms that run in sequence: the rotation (0.24~s per image), the sines XLA evaluates for its tables (0.25~s; the level's rows are 352 samples long, so each child and pulse needs 256 sines for 512 rotated samples) and the traffic of those tables (0.17~s). The final stage is 2.2 times its matrix bound; the remainder is the ramp recurrence on the vector unit, which the microbenchmarks did not isolate. On the v5e the matrix units, about three times slower than the v6e's on a large product against a vector unit only 12\% slower, become the binding unit of levels 0 and 1, and every stage sits between its largest bound and the sum of its bounds (1.5 to 2.2 times the former, 0.6 to 0.8 of the latter), that is, its units overlap partly. The one lever this leaves inside the factorization is the level-2 table generation, which angle-addition tables could cut by about eight times for roughly 0.2~s of the v6e image's 1.7; every other stage is within 1.5 to 2.2 times its largest single bound and below the sum of its bounds.

\paragraph{L4 kernels (CUDA).} On the GPU the natural form of the same two kernels is CUDA, compiled through CuPy. The level kernel loads the window of 32 pulses into shared memory with consecutive threads on consecutive columns, which is the access pattern the memory system serves in full-width transactions, and rotates it in place for each child, the rotation of each thread's samples generated as a geometric sequence. Each thread then computes two adjacent outputs of one pulse with the 44-tap decimation filter as fused multiply-adds; for this phase consecutive threads are consecutive pulses, so no two threads of a group read the same shared-memory bank, and the outputs are staged through shared memory and written row by row. The pulse-axis filter is tiled the same way. The final kernel builds the two ramp tables of a tile in shared memory by the same recurrence and each thread accumulates a 4 by 2 block of pixels; in the sixteen-bit build the tables are converted to float16 and the $2T \times 2T$ product runs on the tensor cores with float32 accumulation. A Triton version of the level kernel through Pallas, with the 16-column windows its shared-memory limit allows, gained one to four percent at sixteen bits and was slower in float32; the CUDA builds are what Table~\ref{tab:cost} reports.

The CUDA code went through seven builds, five of them profiled (Table~\ref{tab:kernels}; the profiles are in the repository). The profile of the second build placed 1.5~s per image in the level kernel, 1.3 in the final stage and 0.5 in the pulse filter. The pulse-major thread mapping of the third build freed the filter reads of bank conflicts but made the global loads and stores of that kernel scattered, and its time doubled; coalesced loads through shared memory, the geometric-sequence rotation and the staged stores brought it to 1.9, 1.7 and 1.4~s in the fourth, fifth and sixth builds. The memory access pattern changed the time by more than the sine count did. The float32 final stage was bound by the latency of its table-building recurrence and by one shared-memory word per fused multiply-add; the 4 by 2 block per thread with interleaved complex tables brought it from 1.3 to 1.0~s. The tensor-core version is 0.4~s faster per image than the float32 one in the whole-image measurement and @@l4.f16tc.loss@@~dB less accurate. The seventh build stores the phase history, every intermediate and the final-stage operands in float16 and accumulates in float32 inside the filters. The float16 storage halves the memory traffic that bounds the level kernel and the pulse filter. It forms the image in @@knew.l4.16.s@@~s against @@knew.l4.32.s@@~s for the float32 build, with @@l4.f16.loss@@~dB more error, and is the float16 configuration of Table~\ref{tab:cost}. The sixth build with the tensor-core final stage and float32 storage is the intermediate setting at @@l4.f16tc.s@@~s.

Against the L4's measured rates (memory copy 246~GB/s; the level kernel's inner mix on resident shared memory 20.2 billion filter outputs per second; the final kernel's inner mix 6.1 trillion fused multiply-adds per second, 41\% of the chip's peak; coalesced stores 252~GB/s against 69 strided), the pulse filter runs at its memory bound, the level kernel at 1.1 times the sum of its arithmetic and memory bounds (1.9 times their maximum; the two overlap only partly at two blocks per multiprocessor), and the float32 final stage at 2.2 times the bound of its own inner mix, the difference being the table-building phase. The remaining structural cost of the float32 build is the 172~GB per image that the level kernel writes and the pulse filter reads back; a streaming fusion of the two could remove about half of it, worth about 0.3~s of the 3.6. The float16 build halves that traffic instead and gains @@l4.f16.gain.s@@~s on the float32 time, which is consistent with the level kernel's time being mostly its arithmetic bound. Float16 storage does not move that bound, because the L4's CUDA cores issue float16 and float32 fused multiply-adds at the same rate (128 per clock per multiprocessor at compute capability 8.9~\cite{cudaguide}). The remaining float16 route on this card is the tensor cores, which would require rewriting the level filters as matrix products in the manner of the TPU kernel.

\section{Polar format on an orbital aperture}
\label{app:pfa}

The implementation consists of three resampling steps and a two-dimensional FFT, each expressed with FFTs, element-wise products or a single matrix product so that the same code runs on all devices. The pulses are first resampled so that the tangent of the look angle in the image plane is uniform in pulse index, which an orbit does not give exactly; this is a 16-tap Kaiser-windowed sinc interpolation along the pulse axis applied as a dense @@pfa.P@@ by @@pfa.P@@ matrix (the form selected on the v6e) or as a sum over 16 gathered rows (the form selected on the L4, the CPU and the v5e; both forms were timed on the L4, the v6e and the CPU). Each pulse is then resampled onto a uniform radial wavenumber grid, and each wavenumber row onto a uniform cross-range grid, by chirp-z transforms~\cite{rabiner1969}, which evaluate the band-limited interpolation on a scaled grid with FFTs and chirps; outputs that would wrap around the band are zeroed. The frequency samples are weighted by $f_c / f_k$ before resampling, because backprojection's uniform weighting of the samples corresponds to a density of $1/k$ in the wavenumber plane (the polar Jacobian) that the uniform spectral weighting of polar format omits. Without this weighting the image's error worsens by about 1.5~dB. It is applied in every reported polar-format image.

Because the grazing angle changes along the aperture, the radial band of each pulse projected onto the image plane drifts from pulse to pulse. The raster covers the union of the bands and so keeps the full spectral support of backprojection, a trapezoid, rather than the inscribed rectangle; in the ground plane the rectangle would hold half the bandwidth, in the vendor's slant plane nearly all of it. The wavenumber grid spacings are set by the native pixel spacings and FFT sizes of @@pfa.nfx@@ by @@pfa.nfy@@, which include a 300~m guard band on every side of the scene; the range and azimuth profiles are tapered to zero within the guard, because the periodic transforms otherwise wrap echoes from beyond the image into its edges (without the guard the edge error was $-5$~dB). The image is cut from the FFT output on the native grid with the carrier phases restored.

\paragraph{Distortion and the final resampling.} The planar model writes the range history of a scatterer at $\mathbf{p}$ as $-\hat{\mathbf{u}}_p \cdot \mathbf{q} + c$; a least-squares fit of $\mathbf{q}$ and $c$ over the aperture to the exact history $\lVert \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{a}_p \rVert - \lVert \mathbf{a}_p \rVert$ gives the position at which polar format focuses the scatterer, $(q_x, q_y + c)$, since a constant range offset is a range shift. The fit residual is below 1~mrad of phase everywhere in the three scenes, so before the final resampling the image is displaced without defocus, by up to @@pfa.shift.panama.az@@ pixels in azimuth and @@pfa.shift.panama.rg@@ in range at the corners of the Panama scene; measured target positions agree with the model to 0.2~pixel. The mapping is fitted by cubic polynomials over the image (residual below $10^{-4}$~pixel).

The image is resampled through the mapping: the spectrum is placed in a raster of twice the pixel density, transformed without the carrier terms, interpolated at the apparent positions with a six-tap Lanczos kernel along each axis, and multiplied by the carriers evaluated at the apparent positions. No separate phase correction is needed; the quadratic phase of about @@pfa.center.quad@@~radians at 211~m seen in the center crop before the resampling is the carrier of the range displacement. The oversampled raster is processed in blocks rather than held in memory. The range transform is done once, and the azimuth transform and the resampling proceed in blocks of 512 output range columns.

The resampled image's error against the reference is $-39$~dB in the central 1024 by 1024 pixels and $-32$~dB over the whole Panama image, growing toward the edges (the rings of Table~\ref{tab:pfaedge} and Figure~\ref{fig:rings} are quarters of the half-width, not this crop). On the full Panama image the resampling kernel (4, 6 and 8 taps) and the oversampling of the intermediate raster (2 and 3 times) change the whole-image error by less than 0.6~dB and the outermost ring, where the displacement is 10 to 18 pixels, by less than 0.4~dB (Table~\ref{tab:pfaedge}); lengthening the pulse interpolator from 16 to 32 or 48 taps, with a narrower Kaiser window, made the error slightly worse. The registration, measured from the cross-spectrum phase, is 0.001~pixel, and the reference's own convergence is more than 30~dB below the residual.

\section{Measurement protocol in detail}
\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{Time and cost.} Device time is measured from the start of the per-collection host work (for the factorized algorithm the float64 geometry of the first two levels, about 0.5~s) to the completion of the image on the device. Each configuration was compiled and run once and then timed once more; the second run is reported, and a few configurations were timed twice more, with a spread below 4\%, so differences of that order between rows are not resolved. 

The pipelined loop ran until at least four images had been formed and at least 40~s (polar format) or 60~s (factorized) had elapsed, whichever came later, except that the exact backprojection loops on the L4 ran two or three images (190 to 495~s), polar format on the v6e two images (up to 215~s) and the CPU factorized loop one image per process (612~s with four processes), and the reported figure is images divided by elapsed time. On the TPUs the pipelined loop overlaps the host work with the device and its throughput slightly exceeds the inverse of the device time; on the L4, whose host has four vCPUs, the factorized loops ran 6 to 11\% slower per image than the device time. 

The CPU instance's utilization was recorded from \texttt{/proc/stat} during every run, and its throughput was also measured with two and four worker processes pinned to disjoint cores, because a single XLA process does not occupy all sixteen hardware threads.

\paragraph{Power.} The L4's draw was sampled from \texttt{nvidia-smi} twice a second during the timed loops. The TPU virtual machines and the CPU instance expose no power counters; the bounds derived for them from published design figures are in Section~\ref{sec:power} and are marked as estimates.

\paragraph{Precision.} The gain fitted per algorithm family is a complex gain for the backprojections and a real one for polar format, whose phase differs from the reference by a smooth screen before the final resampling. Six statistics are computed for each image. The error is the energy of the difference relative to the energy of the reference (dB); this is the figure quoted when a single number is given. The others are the largest single-pixel difference relative to the brightest pixel (dB); the complex coherence between the two images over a 5 by 5 window, as the mean, the 0.1 percentile and the minimum; the amplitude ratio and the phase difference over the brighter half of the pixels, as the 99th percentile and the maximum; and the error in four rings by distance from the scene center. Appendix~\ref{app:stats} tabulates all of them for every configuration whose image was saved. The selected configurations are defined in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}; the implementation forms among which the choice is made are the filter, ramp and resampling forms, and Appendices~\ref{app:tpu} and \ref{app:pfa} give the measurements behind each choice.

\paragraph{CPU details.} The CPU code is generated by XLA from the same source as the accelerator code and has not been compared with a hand-tuned CPU implementation; the polar-format and float64 rows of Table~\ref{tab:cost} are single-process timings. Exact backprojection was run on the CPU only as the float64 reference with 16 times oversampling, which took @@ref.panama.s@@~s on the n2-highmem-48 of Section~\ref{sec:devices}; at about \$1,210 per thousand images it serves only as the reference and is not a candidate configuration.

\paragraph{Reference convergence and exact backprojection.} The reference is a float64 exact backprojection with range profiles oversampled 16 times. Its convergence was measured on a 1024 by 1024 crop by forming the same crop at 8, 16 and 32 times: the 8 times image differs from the 32 times image by @@ref.conv8@@~dB and the 16 times image from the 32 times image by @@ref.conv16@@~dB. At 8 times the convergence error would have been comparable to the differences under study, so the 16 times reference was used; even so, no exact-backprojection error can be measured below the convergence of the reference.

With 16 times oversampled profiles, against the 16 times reference whose interpolation they share, the L4 float32 and float16 images measure @@p.gpu-l4.bp.cuda_fp32_ov16.err@@ and @@p.gpu-l4.bp.cuda_f16_ov16.err@@~dB. At these settings the error of exact backprojection is set by the profile oversampling, approximately $-56$~dB at 8 times and, within the limit set by the convergence of the reference itself, approximately $-69$~dB at 16 times; float16 storage of the profiles adds no measurable error at 8 times, and at 16 times it separates the two shared-interpolation figures by 4~dB, which against the $-69$~dB floor amounts to less than 1~dB. With 16 times oversampling, exact backprojection takes @@t.panama.gpu-l4.bp.cuda_f16_ov16.s@@~s with float16 profiles and @@t.panama.gpu-l4.bp.cuda_fp32_ov16.s@@~s with float32 profiles, which at the instance price is \$@@c16.f16.usd@@ and \$@@c16.fp32.usd@@ per thousand images.

\paragraph{Polar format on each device.} On the v5e the final resampling adds @@pfa.corr.v5e.add@@~s to the @@t.panama.tpu-v5e.pfa.fp32_taps.s@@~s that precede it. The contiguous row gathers of the pulse resampling run nearly a hundred times faster on the TPU than the scattered two-dimensional gathers of the final resampling. A separable approximation of that step, expressible as row and column phase ramps, would avoid the gathers, but the squinted geometry of these collections is not exactly separable in this way. A form of the final resampling that avoids scattered gathers would reduce the TPU's disadvantage in Section~\ref{sec:pfa}; the size of the reduction was not measured. The dense pulse-resampling product (images saved on the L4, the v6e and the CPU) and the gather form (the v5e and the CPU) give images that agree to the precision of Table~\ref{tab:prec}.

\section{Ground truth of the Panama regions}
\label{app:truth}

The identity of the Panama regions used in Figure~\ref{fig:zoom} was checked against Copernicus Sentinel-2 imagery~\cite{sentinel2} (Figure~\ref{fig:eo}). Each pixel of the images formed here was located on the ground from the vendor's SICD header: the pixel's position in the slant plane follows from the scene center point and the row and column unit vectors, and the ground point is the intersection of the line through it along the slant plane's normal with the WGS84 ellipsoid at the scene height. The image corners so computed agree with the corner coordinates in the SICD header to within 15~m. The nearest usable Sentinel-2 acquisition is from 2023-07-23, five days later, at 10~m resolution; a dry-season acquisition of 2024-02-23 is shown as well because clouds cover part of the earlier one. The lock chambers, the quays and container yard of the terminal, and the road beside the vegetation region coincide in the two sensors. The bright target of the ship region, about 180~m long and close to the eastern bank, is not a fixed structure: the Panama Canal is a repeat-collection site in the Umbra catalog, and in the vendor's geocoded products of six other passes, four of them three, four, five and seven days after this collection and two in February 2024, the same water is empty (Figure~\ref{fig:channel}). The pass of 2023-07-25 02:30 UTC repeats the geometry of this collection to within $0.3^\circ$ (azimuth $122.4^\circ$ against $122.1^\circ$, grazing angle $42.6^\circ$ in both, left-looking), which rules out an aspect-dependent return from a fixed object; the other passes span azimuths from $-72^\circ$ to $223^\circ$ and grazing angles from $40^\circ$ to $63^\circ$. The pass of 2023-07-23 14:47 UTC precedes the Sentinel-2 image of that day by 64 minutes, and neither shows anything at the position. The target is therefore a ship in the navigation channel; the Sentinel-2 image of 2024-02-23 shows a different ship nearby. The vendor's geocoded product of this collection places the target inside the outlined crop, which confirms the pixel-to-ground mapping independently of the corner check.

The ship appears on the eastern bank, inland of the channel, as a consequence of its motion during the aperture. A stationary-scene processor places a target with line-of-sight velocity $v_r$ at the position whose Doppler history matches, displaced along azimuth by about $R v_r / V$ (here about 100~m per m/s), and the along-track velocity defocuses it~\cite{raney1971}. Since exact backprojection takes the antenna position of every pulse as data, a target moving at constant velocity $\mathbf{v}$ is formed exactly by backprojecting with the track shifted by $-\mathbf{v} t$, which both focuses it and places it at its position at the aperture center. The ship was assumed to follow the channel axis (bearing $158^\circ$, from the Sentinel-2 water mask) and its speed was swept from $-8$ to $8$~m/s in a tile around it (Figure~\ref{fig:ship}). The sharpness of the tile, the sum of the squared intensities, is single-peaked at $3.0$~m/s (5.8~knots toward the Pacific), where the peak intensity is 10.6~dB above the stationary-scene image and the ship has moved 469 pixels (194~m) along azimuth into the navigation channel; the position of the water was not used in the estimate. A heading $10^\circ$ to the west ($148^\circ$) gives the same speed with an 18\% lower sharpness peak and a compensated position 22~m away. The images compared in Figures~\ref{fig:zoom} and \ref{fig:pixels} are the stationary-scene images, in which the ship appears at the same displaced position under every algorithm; the motion analysis serves only to establish that the target is a ship.
