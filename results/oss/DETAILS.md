# Comparison with open-source implementations


On the Panama collection, FastSAR (https://github.com/saulpingerman/FastSAR) forms the full image in 12.4 s on the 16-vCPU c4d instance and in 2.9 to 3.6 s on the L4, with errors of -54.6 to -61.2 dB. Of the four open-source backprojection implementations compared here, those run on the same CPU instance would need an estimated 4.8 to 69 h for the same image; on the L4, the CUDA backprojection of ISCE3 would need about 27 min, and torchbp did not form a usable image (Table (tab:oss)).

RITSAR (commit 0e36d2e, ported to Python 3 without other modification) is a NumPy library. `bpBasic` is the backprojection function of the MATLAB toolbox of Gorham and Moore, written at the Air Force Research Laboratory and distributed, with modifications, by the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency in its MATLAB_SAR repository (commit 63f0401); it was run in GNU Octave 8.4. ISCE3 (version 0.25.17) implements backprojection in C++ with OpenMP (and in CUDA), and torchbp (commit b371884) is a PyTorch extension with CUDA kernels.

The inputs of the reference image (the float64 exact backprojection of Section (sec:protocol)), namely the Taylor-windowed phase history and the antenna positions, were converted to the input format of each implementation. Each output image was scored against the reference with the error measure of that section. To limit the run time of the slowest implementations, scoring was restricted to three of the four 512 by 512 pixel regions of Figure (fig:zoom), those of the lock, the port and the ship. ISCE3 forms images on its own radar grid, so it was scored on 256 by 256 pixel patches of that grid, against the reference evaluated at the same points, in the lock and port regions and at the scene center.

FastSAR's exact backprojection (`fastsar.backproject`, float64 geometry, linear interpolation of eightfold-oversampled profiles) separates the contribution of the implementation from that of the algorithm. On the same instance it formed the full image in 46 min with all 16 threads busy, at errors of -54.9 to -58.7 dB: against ISCE3, the same algorithm with a different implementation, about 6 times faster and more accurate, and about 220 times slower than FastSAR's factorized backprojection. All CPU times were measured on the instance type of FastSAR's CPU row (c4d-highmem-16, an AMD EPYC 9B45 with 8 cores and 16 vCPUs), each implementation with the whole instance available. RITSAR and `bpBasic` do not parallelize their backprojection and kept about one core busy (106% and 103 to 105% CPU); ISCE3, run with 16 threads, kept about 5.6 cores busy (560%). The scripts, run logs and instance list are in the directories `oss` and `results/oss` of the study repository.

## Polar format. The NGA repository also holds a polar format implementation, `pfa_mem` (Schwartzkopf and Krauss, NGA), which was run unmodified in GNU Octave on the same c4d-highmem-16 instance with the Taylor-windowed phase history of the reference; one function of the repository's support code, `cross`, needed a compatibility version for Octave (a row result for mixed row and column inputs, which the code expects). It formed the full image on its own slant-plane grid (22,779 by 21,598 samples) in 44 s. Its image is not corrected for the planar-wavefront displacement of polar format, so each region was registered to the reference by amplitude cross-correlation (offsets of 4 by 2 and 7 by 4 pixels at the lock and the port, as for the vendor image of Appendix (app:sicd)), resampled onto the reference pixels with its spectrum moved to zero frequency, and scored as the vendor image is, by amplitude statistics and the mean coherence in 5 by 5 pixel windows after removal of a linear phase ramp (Table (tab:osspfa)). The polar format of FastSAR, which removes the displacement in its final resampling onto the reference grid (Appendix (app:pfa)), is scored the same way. Part of the difference between the two comes from the resampling of the NGA image and from the displacement varying within a region, which a single offset per region does not remove.

## RITSAR. As published, RITSAR's images of the three regions have errors of -21.3 to -24.2 dB at its default sixfold range oversampling and -25.3 to -27.3 dB at sixteenfold. The excess error comes from two indexing defects: the range axis of the oversampled profiles, built with `linspace` over N_{fft points spanning the full extent, has a spacing too large by one part in N_{fft}-1, and the frequency axis is centered on the assumption of an even sample count, which the 14,399 frequency samples of the Panama collection do not satisfy. RITSAR holds the oversampled profiles of all pulses at once (about 64 GB at sixteenfold), more than the instance's 126 GB holds with temporaries, so for timing it was called on blocks of 4,096 pulses whose images were added; the lock region took 384 s.

## `bpBasic`. The improvement of 12 to 15 dB from doubling the FFT length is consistent with an error dominated by the linear interpolation of the range profiles. At each FFT length the error is 14 to 19 dB above that of the exact backprojection of Table (tab:cost), plausibly because `bpBasic` references the phase of its profiles to the lowest frequency of the band rather than its center, so that the profiles rotate between samples. Scoring the FastSAR CPU, L4 float32 and TPU v6e three-pass images (Table (tab:cost)) against the `bpBasic` images instead of the reference changes their errors by at most 2.3 dB. In Octave the lock region took 499 s at the default FFT length and 604 s at 2^{18}.

## ISCE3. ISCE3's backprojection takes range-compressed data on an absolute delay axis together with an orbit. Its input was the windowed data of the reference, range compressed with an FFT length of 32,768 (2.28 times the sample count). It was run with its own 8-tap Knab interpolator, all 15,186 pulses in the integration and a constant Doppler centroid matching the 28 deg squint of the collection. ISCE3 reported that some of its geometry iterations did not converge, although no output pixel was invalid. The estimate in Table (tab:oss) rests on a mean time of 10.5 s per 256 by 256 pixel patch with 16 threads. The CUDA version on the L4 took 3.4 s for the same patches and 15.7 s for a 1024 by 1024 pixel patch at the scene center; its images differ from those of the CPU version by -53 to -55 dB and carry the same ramp and residual (-52.2 dB at the center, -31.5 and -31.9 dB at the port and lock after the ramp is removed).

The correlation coefficient between the amplitude images of ISCE3 and those of the reference is 0.9995 to 1.0000. Most of the complex difference is a linear phase ramp of -0.0015 rad per pixel in range and -0.0007 rad per pixel in azimuth. The ramp is the same in the three patches and most likely reflects a difference in the spectral centering of the two images. With the ramp removed, the center patch has an error of -54.9 dB.

The port and lock patches keep a residual (-31.5 and -31.9 dB) spread over the pixels in proportion to their amplitude. The cause of this residual was not identified. The output positions of ISCE3 and the points at which the reference was evaluated agree to 4 nm in height. A reference formed with separate transmit and receive positions, instead of the midpoint antenna model, gives the same error. An FFT length of 65,536 instead of 32,768 leaves the residual unchanged, and the azimuth and range spectra of the two images agree to within 0.4 and 0.07 dB, respectively.

## torchbp. torchbp takes range-compressed data on a single absolute delay axis for all pulses. The range to the Panama scene center changes by 12 km over the aperture, 1.9 times the 6.4 km range extent c/(2\Delta f) set by the frequency sample spacing. Each pulse was therefore range compressed with an FFT length of 32,768 and placed at its own offset in a delay buffer spanning that change. The buffer occupies 10.8 GiB; at an FFT length of 65,536 it would occupy 21.5 GiB of the card's 22 GiB.

torchbp's exact backprojection has an error of 0.0 dB in all three regions. It evaluates the distance from each pixel to the antenna in float32, and at the 744 km range of the collection adjacent float32 values are 6 cm apart, about two wavelengths, so the computed two-way phase is essentially random. A NumPy reimplementation of the same formula in float64, applied to a 128 by 128 pixel crop of the same buffer in the lock region, forms the image (error -28.3 dB, the level expected from linear interpolation of profiles oversampled 2.28 times). The same reimplementation in float32 reproduces the failure, so the failure is attributed to the float32 distance. FastSAR evaluates the geometry in float64 and only the offsets within tiles in float32, which avoids this loss of precision. The full image took 15.2 s, of which 2.3 s was range compression.

The factorized backprojection of torchbp did not run on the full image: with the buffer resident, it required a further 5.9 GiB when only 4.5 GiB was free. On the three regions it returned images of zeros in 0.7 s each. On the crop, its exact backprojection onto a polar grid also has an error of 0.0 dB. No speed ratio against FastSAR is given for torchbp, since its output is not a usable image; its 15.2 s measures only the cost of the computation.
